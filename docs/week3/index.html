What I learned

Describe your design: why these routers, LANs and links, and how you chose the addresses.

Four routers form a ring, with one host on each router's LAN. The ring gives two physical paths between routers, while the static routes deliberately choose one. The LANs use 10.86.0.0/24, 10.86.16.0/24, 10.86.32.0/24 and 10.86.33.0/24 inside my 10.86.0.0/16 block. Separate /30 networks number the four router links. This layout makes the difference between physical redundancy and a usable backup route visible.

Which route summarises several LANs? Which networks does it cover, why is it correct, and what else would it attract if you added a network inside its range?

On r1 and r2, 10.86.32.0/23 covers the h3 and h4 LANs, 10.86.32.0/24 and 10.86.33.0/24, without covering h1 or h2. r1 sends that summary to r4; r2 sends it to r3. The destination router's connected /24 wins by longest prefix match. If another 10.86.32.x or 10.86.33.x network were added, this summary would attract its traffic too, so it would need a real route beyond the next hop rather than merely matching on paper.

When you took a link down, what happened, and why couldn't your routes recover even though another path existed? What would have recovered?

When the r1-r4 link goes down, r1's chosen next hop for the 10.86.32.0/23 summary is no longer reachable. The ring still has an r1-r2-r3 path, but these static routes do not discover or select it. A routing protocol with failure detection and reconvergence, or a planned backup route with suitable metrics, could restore connectivity.

In your network, what was the control plane? Who worked out the routes, and what does that mean for a network of 40 routers?

The static route plan in network.yaml serves as the control-plane decision; build_routed.py then checked and installed those decisions. Linux forwarded packets using the resulting tables. For 40 routers, hand-maintained paths would be hard to keep consistent when links fail or addresses change, so a routing protocol or controller would need to compute and update them from a current view of the network.
